1 Background

Here we are looking at a multifactorial differential gene expression analysis for developing coral embryos at 3 developmental stages (4 hours post fertilization , 9 hours post fertilization, and 14 hours post fertiliation). Embryos at each stage were exposed to either a control, low, mid, or high concentration of PVC leachate. This work aims to understand sublethal effects of microplastic pollution on coral reefs.

1.1 Inputs

  • gcm_filt.csv is the unfiltered gene count matrix for 61 samples (two outlier samples were already removed).
  • metadata_filt.csv is the metadata for 61 samples

1.2 Outputs

  • volcano_facet.png

VST compresses the high-end of the count distribution and expands the low-end, making the variance roughly constant across the range. The resulting values are on an approximate log2 scale but not exact log2(count + 1) — VST is more nuanced, especially for low counts. A range of 5 to 15 corresponds roughly to raw counts ranging from 2³² to 2¹⁵ ≈ 32 to 32,000, so it’s realistic for RNA-seq gene expression data.

5 DESeq LRT test

The likelihood ratio test whether leachate significantly improves the model, after accounting for hpf. Full model: ~ hpf + leachate Reduced model: ~ hpf In other words: Are there any genes whose expression is significantly explained by leachate, beyond what hpf already explains? This is a global test — it evaluates whether any effect of leachate is present (e.g. multiple levels in a factor, or a continuous dose effect), but it doesn’t tell you which direction the effect goes (up/down), or which level of leachate is responsible.

dds_lrt <- DESeq(dds, test = "LRT", reduced = ~ hpf)

plotDispEsts(dds_lrt)

This compares the full model ~ hpf + leachate to the reduced model ~ hpf and genes with significant p-values can be interpreted as ones that are responding to leachate, regardless of direction (up or down) or level (low, mid, high). Another way to view it is what does the + leachate do that isn’t explained by hpf alone?

This design is to detect any effect of leachate — even if small or complex — while controlling for hpf.

In the results summary, 12 genes show significant evidence of being affected by leachate (FDR < 0.1), according to the LRT model.

Half the genes (~5,111) were filtered out due to low mean counts.

Many of our p-values are close to zero, but LFCs are small — which can happen when there are consistent but subtle differences with low variance. These 12 genes are significant across all leachate levels and across all developmental stages!

6.0.3 Summarize leachate results

LRT is a global test The LRT asks, “Is there any evidence that leachate (as a whole) explains expression differences?” It considers all levels together (low, mid, high), in one model comparison. Genes with subtle or non-monotonic effects — say, up in mid but down in high — might show up as significant in the LRT.

Wald is a single, focused contrast The Wald test asks, “Is the log2 fold change between leachate_high and control significantly different from zero?” It’s much stricter; a gene must have enough separation in just that one contrast, and low enough variability, to pass the threshold. If the gene is mildly different in high vs control, but stronger in mid vs control, the wald_high contrast might not detect it.

Multiple testing and power The LRT runs one test per gene, and shares power across all leachate levels. The Wald test runs one test per gene per contrast, and each test stands alone. So Wald is more prone to Type II error (false negatives) when changes are small or variance is high.

Practical Example: Imagine a gene behaves like this:

hpf control low mid high
4 10 11 30 12
9 10 9 29 10
14 11 12 32 11

LRT: - sees a strong effect of leachate overall (mid is way up)

Wald: - mid vs control: may be significant ✅ - high vs control: likely not significant ❌ - low vs control: also not significant ❌

So you’d get: LRT: gene flagged Wald (high): gene not flagged

✅ What You Should Do - Keep the 12 genes from the LRT — they are likely affected by leachate. - Use the Wald results to narrow down which leachate levels drive the change.

# DESeq LRT test
The likelihood ratio test whether leachate significantly improves the model, after accounting for hpf.
Full model: ~ hpf + leachate
Reduced model: ~ hpf
In other words: Are there any genes whose expression is significantly explained by leachate, beyond what hpf already explains? This is a global test — it evaluates whether any effect of leachate is present (e.g. multiple levels in a factor, or a continuous dose effect), but it doesn’t tell you which direction the effect goes (up/down), or which level of leachate is responsible.
```{r}
dds_lrt <- DESeq(dds, test = "LRT", reduced = ~ hpf)
```

::: Callout-note
This compares the full model `~ hpf + leachate` to the reduced model `~ hpf` and genes with significant p-values can be interpreted as ones that are responding to `leachate`, regardless of direction (up or down) or level (low, mid, high). Another way to view it is what does the `+ leachate` do that isn't explained by `hpf` alone? 

This design is to detect any effect of `leachate` — even if small or complex — while controlling for `hpf`.

These 12 genes are significant across all leachate levels and across all developmental stages!

### Summarize leachate results
LRT is a global test
The LRT asks, "Is there any evidence that leachate (as a whole) explains expression differences?" It considers all levels together (low, mid, high), in one model comparison. Genes with subtle or non-monotonic effects — say, up in mid but down in high — might show up as significant in the LRT.

Wald is a single, focused contrast
The Wald test asks, "Is the log2 fold change between leachate_high and control significantly different from zero?" It’s much stricter; a gene must have enough separation in just that one contrast, and low enough variability, to pass the threshold. If the gene is mildly different in high vs control, but stronger in mid vs control, the wald_high contrast might not detect it.

Multiple testing and power
The LRT runs one test per gene, and shares power across all leachate levels. The Wald test runs one test per gene per contrast, and each test stands alone. So Wald is more prone to Type II error (false negatives) when changes are small or variance is high.

Practical Example:
Imagine a gene behaves like this:

| hpf | control | low | mid | high |
| --- | ------- | --- | --- | ---- |
| 4   | 10      | 11  | 30  | 12   |
| 9   | 10      | 9   | 29  | 10   |
| 14  | 11      | 12  | 32  | 11   |


LRT: 
- sees a strong effect of leachate overall (mid is way up)

Wald:
- mid vs control: may be significant ✅
- high vs control: likely not significant ❌
- low vs control: also not significant ❌

So you'd get:
LRT: gene flagged
Wald (high): gene not flagged

✅ What You Should Do
- Keep the 12 genes from the LRT — they are likely affected by leachate.
- Use the Wald results to narrow down which leachate levels drive the change.
